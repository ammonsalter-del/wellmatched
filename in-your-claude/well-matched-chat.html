First faculty post · European business and management schools

See your record through a hiring committee's eyes.

It reads your materials against each department, and shows where you match best, and why.

Free, open source and private. This version runs inside your own Claude chat, on your own Claude account. The tool itself keeps nothing.

This is for PhD students and postdocs looking for their first teaching and research post at a business or management school in Europe. It is an AI tool that assesses your materials the way a shortlisting committee does, then shows how well you match each department you are considering. It is intended to be supportive and developmental, and it is a prototype. Please try it and give feedback to improve it.

What you get

  • for each department you name, a score out of 100 and a reading of how its committee might see you, square by square;
  • what might hold you back there, and what could change it;
  • other schools worth a look, including wild cards you may not have considered;
  • a report to keep, with every piece of material used.

Paste what you know about each department, or use the message in Step 2 to have your Claude chat find it.

How it works

Well Matched uses a team of Claude agents that read your materials and check each reading, within fixed rules. Like any AI, it can make mistakes.

It shows how a committee is likely to assess you. The FAQs say why, and where it falls short.

The aim is to help you apply where you fit, rather than everywhere. Each application costs you time, and a poor fit costs the committee time too. The tool does not write your application. It shows how a committee might read it, and what you might say to each department.

It stops at the shortlist. Careers take many paths, and where you fit now may not be where you work later. People move, take breaks and grow as teachers. Research takes time, publishing has an element of luck, and a journal's standing is not a perfect guide to a paper's quality.

FAQs about Well Matched

Why use this? You are about to apply for your first post, and you cannot see how a committee will read your file. Most people apply everywhere, which costs time and rarely works. Well Matched reads your materials and each department's own pages the way a shortlisting committee is likely to, and tells you where you fit, where you do not yet, and what to say to each one.

Why should I trust its reading? Committees make decisions under uncertainty. A complex cocktail of factors shapes their choices, and within it status hierarchies, journal lists and school rankings act as weak signals. Well Matched draws on those signals to show how a committee is likely to read a record.

Where does it fall short? It is AI, and it makes mistakes. The search can miss people on a school's pages, and the readings vary a little from one run to the next, so a point or two of difference means nothing. It knows European business schools and only the shortlisting stage; interviews and local preferences come later. Check what it found before you read its verdict, and weigh it alongside the advice of your supervisor and colleagues.

How well has it been tested? Not extensively. Well Matched is a prototype. The rules come from the developer's experience and have been checked against invented candidates and by beta-testers with their own materials, but not against real hiring outcomes. Comments are welcome.

Is this a judgement of me? No. You are talented and capable, and the tool is there to tell you how others read materials about you, which is a different thing. Academic careers are long, and as yours develops the judgements will shift from where you trained to what you have done. Where people start in the academic labour market is rarely where they end, and there are many pathways. The developer finished a DPhil at the Science Policy Research Unit, with no publications. It was a wonderful training, but it would have got a poor match at many business schools.

What if part of it does not feel supportive? The tool is meant to be developmental and supportive, since applying for a first academic post is stressful enough. The developer has tried to hold it to that tone, but AI models can drift. Please remember that the readings are generated by AI, which is a machine.

Does it allow for bias? No. Well Matched makes no allowance for the biases a committee may hold about nationality, race, gender or any other personal characteristic, what the law often calls protected characteristics. Since AI is trained on human data, its reading may carry implicit human biases of its own. The tool does not ask for your personal characteristics, and it is best not to give it any. It does take some account of language skills and reasons for moving, but these count only at the margin. In the developer's experience, academic committees try to be fair. For career breaks it follows the same principle as the European Research Council, extending the window of assessment by a year for every significant break.

Why does it score whether senior staff would warm to me? Committees tend to favour people like themselves, so the tool takes account of it. It is a small part of the overall evaluation.

Why does it name real people? The readings name a department's staff from the school's own pages and their publication records, which are public. Nothing about anyone is kept.

What if my PhD is from outside Europe? Your PhD school is placed from worldwide rankings, so it counts in the same way as any other. The departments it reads, and the places and wild cards it suggests, are European business schools, which is what the tool knows.

What if my PhD school is not in the rankings? PhD training is shown as No information and left out of the score, the status square is left to Claude's reading of the department, and no places or wild cards can be suggested from the rankings.

Was personal data used to build it? No personal data or real cases were used in making the tool. It rests on public rankings and lists, the developer's own knowledge and experience, comments from beta-testers on their experience of using it with their own materials, and many runs with fictional personas.

Is this a university tool? No. Nothing from Warwick Business School went into it, the university does not use it, and no university should: it is for students, not committees. It endorses no list or ranking. It shares one person's experience of how applicants are assessed, gathered at several universities over many rounds of shortlisting for different roles, and it is informed by research on the topic.

Is my material used to train the AI? Not by default. On this website your material goes to Anthropic through its API, and Anthropic says it does not use what is sent that way to train its models unless you choose to let it. In the versions that run through a Claude account, it depends on the account's setting, Help improve our AI models, under Privacy. Anthropic sets this out in its page on model training, its commercial terms for the API and its note on the account setting. The tool itself keeps nothing, and your key, if you ask the page to remember it, stays only in this browser.

What counts as a significant career break, and why it is asked

A significant career break is six months or more away from research, for example parental leave or a health issue. Committees tend to judge a record against the years since a candidate started their PhD, so the tool takes a year off for each break before it judges yours. This follows the same principle as the European Research Council, which extends its eligibility windows for documented breaks. You do not have to say why. The tool adds no credit for a break; it only stops the time counting against you.

Step 2

The departments you are considering

Finding each department's staff in your chat. Copy this message into your Claude chat, fill in the two parts in square brackets, and send it. Then paste what Claude finds into "The department's people" for that department, check it, and correct anything wrong.

Name each department you are thinking of applying to. For each one, paste its advert and staff information, or copy the message above into your Claude chat and paste what it finds. Check it and correct anything wrong. For each department:

  1. Name the school, the department and the country, and give the start date of the post.
  2. If there is a job advert, paste it in full, including any essential or desirable criteria. The tool works without one, from the department's people and pages.
  3. Paste what you can find about the department's people. Open the staff page, select all (Ctrl+A or Cmd+A), copy and paste it in. Then add the profile pages of three to five assistant and associate professors hired or promoted in the last few years: their research, publications and where they did their PhD matter most. Add also the pages of two or three senior professors in your area: their research, publications, any journals they edit and where they did their PhD. They sit on the committee and will read your job market paper. Paste whole pages, not the parts that suit you.
  4. If you know anything about the school's probation or tenure requirements, from its published rules or from people there, add it. Part of it is fine. The tool works without it.
  5. If someone in the department knows you or your work, say who. Committees sometimes hear from a colleague who can speak about a candidate personally, and this helps the tool judge whether anyone there might do that for you. If you know no one there, say so. That is common.

The more you add about each department, its staff, their research and its recent hires, the better the tool will work. Add as many departments as you like, but each one adds to the time the run takes and to your Claude plan's usage.

A note on status hierarchies

Journal lists and school ranking tables are status hierarchies built on reputation: what people in the field believe, not what is true. Well Matched does not endorse them, and they are unreliable for judging individual people, departments or even universities. Committees are made up of people, just like you. Hiring early-career academics is hard, and in the face of uncertainty committees often fall back on weak signals of status. They may not consult the lists or ranking tables directly, but they are highly likely to be aware of them. Well Matched draws on these hierarchies to show how a committee is likely to read a record, not how it should be read. It looks only at shortlisting, the first stage; later stages bring in other factors, such as the presentation, interviews and local preferences.
