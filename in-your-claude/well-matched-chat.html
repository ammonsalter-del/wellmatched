First faculty post · European business schools

See your record through a hiring committee's eyes.

It reads your materials against each department, and shows where you match best, and why.

Free, open source and private. The developer sees nothing you enter, and nothing it tells you. This version runs inside your own Claude chat, on your own Claude account. There is no server and nothing is kept.

This is for PhD students and postdocs looking for their first teaching and research post at a business school in Europe. It reads your materials the way a shortlisting committee does, then shows how well you match each department you are considering.

What you get

  • for each department you name, a score out of 100 and a reading of how its committee might see you, square by square;
  • what might hold you back there, and what could change it;
  • other schools worth a look, including wild cards you may not have considered;
  • a report to keep, with every piece of material used.

Paste what you know about each department, or use the message in Step 2 to have your Claude chat find it.

How it works

An orchestrated multi-agent AI system: Claude agents read and check, within set rules. AI can make mistakes.

It reflects experience, not an ideal. It is designed to show how hiring committees tend to behave, not how they ought to.

The aim is to help you apply to places that suit you, rather than everywhere. Applications cost you time, and a misaligned one costs the committee time too. This tool does not write or polish your application. It tells you what a committee is likely to see.

It stops at the shortlist. Every academic career pathway is different. Where you place now may not be where you work in the future. People move institutions, take career breaks, research takes time, publishing is hard and has some random elements, journal quality is not a perfect proxy for paper quality, and your teaching skills can develop.

Career breaks since you started your PhD (optional)
What counts as a significant career break, and why we ask

A significant career break is six months or more away from research, for example maternity or parental leave or a health issue. Committees tend to judge a record against the years since a candidate started their PhD: the more years, the higher the expectations. A significant career break should not count against you, so the tool takes a year off for each one before it judges your record. You do not have to say why.

This is not positive discrimination, which in most of Europe is unlawful in hiring, or allowed only in very specific circumstances. In the UK, an employer may favour a candidate because of a protected characteristic only as a tie-breaker between candidates who are as qualified as each other, and never as a set policy (Equality Act 2010, section 159; GOV.UK, Positive action in the workplace: guidance for employers). The tool adds no credit for a break. It only stops the time counting against you.

Be realistic. This is to give the tool a sense of your level of expectations.

Step 2

The departments you are considering

Finding each department's staff in your chat. Copy this message into your Claude chat, fill in the two parts in square brackets, and send it. Then paste what Claude finds into "The department's people" for that department, check it, and correct anything wrong.

Name each department you are thinking of applying to. For each one, paste its advert and staff information, or copy the message above into your Claude chat and paste what it finds. Check it and correct anything wrong. For each department:

  1. Name the school, the department and the country, and give the start date of the post.
  2. If there is a job advert, paste it in full, including any essential or desirable criteria. The tool works without one, from the department's people and pages.
  3. Paste what you can find about the department's people. Open the staff page, select all (Ctrl+A or Cmd+A), copy and paste it in. Then add the profile pages of three to five assistant and associate professors hired or promoted in the last few years: their research, publications and where they did their PhD matter most. Add also the pages of two or three senior professors in your area: their research, publications, any journals they edit and where they did their PhD. They sit on the committee and will read your job market paper. Paste whole pages, not the parts that suit you.
  4. If you know anything about the school's probation or tenure requirements, from its published rules or from people there, add it. Part of it is fine. The tool works without it.
  5. If someone in the department knows you or your work, say who. Committees sometimes hear from a colleague who can speak about a candidate personally, and this helps the tool judge whether anyone there might do that for you. If you know no one there, say so. That is common.

The more you add about each department, its staff, their research and its recent hires, the better the tool will work. Add as many departments as you like, but each one adds to the time the run takes and to your Claude plan's usage.

Sources

Well Matched draws on published journal lists and school rankings. The journal buckets (World leading, Leading, Excellent, Strong) and the school scores are Well Matched's own, and no grade or rank from any list is shown or kept. If you think a journal or school is placed wrongly, please take it up with the list or ranking makers; when their lists change, Well Matched will follow. The development manual explains how the sources are combined, under How the score is made and The school data.

A note on status hierarchies

Journal lists and school ranking tables are status hierarchies built on reputation: what people in the field believe, not what is true. We do not endorse them, and they are unreliable for judging individual people, departments or even universities. Committees are made up of people, just like you. Hiring early-career academics is hard, and in the face of uncertainty committees often fall back on weak signals of status. They may not consult the lists or ranking tables directly, but they are highly likely to be aware of them. Well Matched draws on these hierarchies to show how a committee is likely to read a record, not how it should be read. It looks only at shortlisting, the first stage; later stages bring in other factors, such as the presentation, interviews and local preferences. See the development manual, under The limits of the tool and Related research, for further details.
